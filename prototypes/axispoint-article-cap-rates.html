Cap rates, NOI, and the numbers that actually matter

ZR
Zachary Russell
April 1, 2025
7 min read

If you have spent any time looking at commercial real estate, you have probably heard the terms cap rate and NOI thrown around as if they explain everything. They do not. But they do explain a lot — and understanding what they actually measure, and what they do not, will save you from making decisions based on numbers that have been presented to flatter the deal rather than describe it.

This is not a textbook definition. This is how we actually use these numbers when we are evaluating an asset.

What NOI actually is

Net Operating Income is the income a property generates after you subtract operating expenses but before debt service. That last part matters. NOI does not tell you what you take home after your mortgage payment. It tells you what the property earns on its own, independent of how you financed it.

Key term
Net Operating Income (NOI)
Gross rental income minus vacancy minus operating expenses. Does not include mortgage payments, depreciation, or capital expenditures.

The problem with NOI is that it is only as good as the assumptions behind it. Sellers will show you NOI based on their reported numbers — and those numbers are almost always optimistic. They will use below-market vacancy rates, exclude expenses that should be included, and present income at market rate even when units are not actually at market.

When we underwrite a deal, we rebuild NOI from scratch. We use market-rate vacancy, current replacement cost insurance, full property taxes at market value, and normalized operating expenses based on what we know about comparable assets. That number is almost always lower than what the seller presents. That gap is where most acquisition mistakes happen.

We do not underwrite to a best case. We underwrite to what we can defend when things do not go exactly to plan.

What a cap rate actually tells you

A capitalization rate is the ratio of NOI to purchase price. If a property generates $100,000 in NOI and you buy it for $1,000,000, the cap rate is 10 percent. It is a shorthand for yield — how much income the asset produces relative to its cost, assuming you paid cash.

Cap rates are primarily useful as a market signal. When cap rates are low, investors are willing to accept less income relative to price — usually because they expect appreciation or rent growth to make up the difference. When cap rates are high, investors are demanding more income relative to price — usually because the asset or market carries more risk.

Key term
Capitalization Rate (Cap Rate)
NOI divided by purchase price. A 5% cap rate on a $1M property means the asset generates $50,000 in NOI annually. Lower cap rates generally indicate higher demand or lower perceived risk.

Where people get into trouble is treating the cap rate as a complete picture. It is not. It tells you the yield at the time of purchase based on current NOI. It does not tell you what the NOI will be in two years, what the market will do, or whether the property has deferred maintenance that will eat into that income.

The numbers sellers manipulate most

When you are reviewing a seller's pro forma, these are the line items to scrutinize most carefully:

  • Vacancy: Sellers often use trailing occupancy rather than market vacancy. If a property has been running at 97 percent occupied, they will project 97 percent. We underwrite to market vacancy, which in most Texas markets is closer to 5 to 8 percent.
  • Management fees: Self-managed properties often show no management fee. If you are going to hire a property manager, that cost belongs in the underwrite.
  • Insurance: Properties are frequently insured below replacement cost. We use current replacement cost pricing, which has increased substantially in recent years.
  • Property taxes: In Texas, assessed values reset on sale. A property being sold at a significant premium to its current assessed value will have a higher tax bill after closing than the seller's numbers show.

A note on Texas property taxes: This is one of the most commonly overlooked line items in Texas acquisitions. If you are buying a property at a price significantly above its current assessed value, underwrite taxes at the new purchase price from day one. Do not use the seller's tax figure.

What actually drives value

Cap rate and NOI tell you where an asset is. What matters for a value-add investment is where it is going and what it will take to get there.

The real analysis is in the spread between current NOI and stabilized NOI after a defined capital improvement program. If a property is currently generating $80,000 in NOI but can generate $120,000 after $300,000 in renovations, the question is whether the cost of getting there is justified by the increase in asset value at the other end.

That is the underwriting we do on every deal. Not just what the asset earns today, but what it can earn under a defined business plan, at what cost, over what timeline, and with what risk if things do not go as modeled.

Cap rates and NOI are the starting point. The analysis is everything that comes after.
